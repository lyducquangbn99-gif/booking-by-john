A bill of lading and a sea waybill both record an ocean shipment, but they do not perform the same commercial function. Choosing the wrong instruction can delay document release, cargo delivery, payment, or transfer between buyer and seller.

Terminology and legal effect can vary by carrier, jurisdiction, contract, and document wording. This practical overview is not legal advice; confirm the chosen form with the carrier, bank, buyer, seller, and qualified adviser where necessary.

## What a bill of lading does

A bill of lading commonly serves as evidence of the contract of carriage and as a receipt for goods. A negotiable bill can also function as a document of title, allowing rights to delivery to be transferred by the appropriate endorsement and delivery of the document.

Carriers may issue original paper bills or approved electronic versions. When originals are used, cargo release normally depends on presentation or surrender under the carrier's procedure. "Telex release" is an operational term often used when originals have been surrendered at one location and the carrier authorizes release elsewhere; it is not a separate transport document.

## What a sea waybill does

A sea waybill is generally a non-negotiable transport document. It identifies the named consignee, and cargo is released according to the carrier's identity and release checks rather than presentation of an original negotiable bill.

This can simplify document movement when the parties know and trust each other and no document-of-title function is needed. It does not remove the need for accurate shipper, consignee, notify-party, cargo, freight, and customs information.

## When the choice matters most

Consider a negotiable bill when control of the goods must be retained until payment or another condition is met, when goods may be sold in transit, or when a bank or documentary-credit transaction requires it. Confirm the bank's exact document wording before shipment; correcting a document later may take time and incur fees.

A sea waybill may suit established account relationships, related companies, prepaid transactions, or shipments where fast document transmission is useful and title transfer is unnecessary. It should not be selected simply because the vessel may arrive before couriered originals.

## Details to confirm before shipping instructions

- Which party may select the document type under the sales and carriage contracts
- Whether a bank, letter of credit, or collection requires originals
- Correct legal names and addresses of shipper and consignee
- Whether the bill must be negotiable and how many originals are required
- Freight terms and who controls release
- Carrier deadlines, amendment rules, and release procedure
- Destination customs and identification requirements

Never rely on an informal label alone. Read the carrier's draft carefully and ask every controlling party to approve it before issuance.

## Common avoidable problems

Typical issues include choosing a sea waybill despite a bank requirement, couriering originals too late, inconsistent consignee details, requesting release without authority, and confusing a house document with the carrier's master document. A forwarder may issue a house bill while the carrier issues a master bill; both document chains must be coordinated.
